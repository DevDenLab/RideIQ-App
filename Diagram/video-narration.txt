[OPEN ON: the RideIQ app on a phone, a route drawn across an Edmonton map]

Meet RideIQ — a navigation and ride-hailing app built entirely from scratch.
No Google Maps, no paid APIs. Just open-source maps, machine learning, and a
production-grade cloud pipeline. Let's walk through how it all fits together.

[CUT TO: the full architecture diagram, four zones left to right]

At the highest level, RideIQ has four parts: the phone app, a CI/CD pipeline,
a cloud server, and the backend "brain." Follow one request and it all makes sense.

[HIGHLIGHT: the phone / Client zone]

It starts on your phone. The Android app draws its map from free OpenStreetMap
tiles and talks to the server over simple web requests. You type a destination and
it suggests real addresses as you type. You pick Drive or Walk, and up to three
routes appear as cards — the fastest one recommended, the slower ones greyed out
with how much longer they take, plus the fare and your arrival time.

[HIGHLIGHT: nginx and the blue/green containers on the EC2 host]

Your request travels to a cloud server on Amazon EC2. The front door is nginx,
which forwards it to whichever app container is live. There are always two —
blue and green — but only one serves traffic at a time. The other is a spare,
ready for instant rollback.

[HIGHLIGHT: the backend brain — routing engine and ML models]

Inside that container is the brain. First, the routing engine. A road network is
just a graph — intersections are points, streets are connections weighted by length.
An algorithm called A-star finds the shortest path, then re-runs while penalizing
roads it already used, which is how it produces genuinely different alternatives.
For walking, it uses a separate footpath graph and draws a dashed green line.

[HIGHLIGHT: the stack of ML models]

Then the machine-learning models price the trip. Fifteen models power eight features.
Your time of arrival comes from an ensemble of linear regression, a random forest,
and a neural network. Fare estimation compares your trip to similar past trips.
Other models predict cancellation risk, detect fraud, cluster the city into surge
zones with K-means, and even model a driver's shift with a Hidden Markov Model.
The results come back, and your phone shows the route.

[HIGHLIGHT: the CI/CD pipeline zone]

Now, how does new code get here safely? Every push to GitHub triggers a pipeline.
It builds a Docker image, stores it in a registry, then connects to the server and
performs a blue-green deploy: it starts the new version, health-checks it, and only
switches traffic once it's proven healthy. If anything's wrong, users never see it —
and rolling back is just redeploying the previous version.

[CUT TO: the app — start a trip, marker moving, arrival]

So how do you actually use it? Set your pickup and destination, choose drive or walk,
pick a route, and tap Start trip. The map follows your live GPS, a voice reads each
turn — "in two hundred meters, turn left" — and when you reach your destination,
it tells you: you have arrived.

[CLOSE ON: the RideIQ logo]

That's RideIQ — open maps, real machine learning, and a deployment pipeline that
ships new features without ever taking the app down.
